# Frosthold Writing Craft Database

## How to Use This File
Quick reference for procedural content generation. Find your content type, apply the techniques.
Each entry is actionable: "Do X" not "X is important." Bad/good examples included where they help.
This file consolidates and reorganizes content from WRITING_CRAFT_GUIDE.md and AI_WRITING_TELLS.md, plus additional research.

## QUICK RULES (check before every pool entry)
1. Specific > abstract. "Three rounds left" not "running low."
2. Action > contemplation. Show what they DO, not what they think.
3. Fragments are fine. Real people don't speak in complete sentences.
4. One detail > three adjectives. Pick the one that matters most.
5. Subtext > statement. Imply the horror. Let the reader's imagination work.
6. Contractions in speech. Always. "Don't" not "do not."
7. No AI tells. Check the anti-patterns list.
8. Each entry should pass the "could this describe only ONE person?" test.
9. The mundane detail next to the horrifying detail. That's the contrast that works.
10. If you can cut a word without losing meaning, cut it.

## ATMOSPHERE & ENVIRONMENT

### Creating Dread
Dread is anticipation of something terrible that hasn't arrived yet. It is the strongest of the three fear modes because the reader's imagination does the work.

**Techniques:**
- **Delay the reveal.** The longer you withhold the threat, the worse the reader imagines it. Every sentence before the reveal should add one more wrong detail, not advance toward resolution.
- **Accumulate wrongness.** Don't drop one scary thing into a normal scene. Drop five almost-normal things that are each slightly off. A clock running backward. Food that tastes metallic. A shadow in the wrong direction. Individually dismissible; collectively, dread.
- **Use long sentences.** Dread lives in winding, comma-heavy prose that delays resolution. The reader cannot skim -- they must stay in the discomfort. "The corridor stretched ahead of them, the overhead lights cycling through their dim amber rotation, each pool of light smaller than the last, the shadows between them deeper, until the final light -- the one nearest the pressure door -- flickered."
- **Make the environment an active force.** The setting should press in, resist, deceive. A corridor that seems shorter than it should be. Air that tastes metallic. Silence where there should be sound. In Frosthold: the cold is not weather, it is a predator. The planet is not geology, it is anatomy.
- **Restrict information.** Tight POV forces the reader to share the character's limited knowledge. They cannot see around corners. They hear sounds they cannot identify. Restricted information is the engine of dread.
- **Let characters explain it away.** Denial is more unsettling than fear. "It's just the wind" is not stupidity -- it is a survival mechanism protecting the mind. Show rational people constructing plausible explanations for impossible things.

**Bad:** "The hallway was terrifying and full of dread."
**Good:** "The hallway lights were on a timer. She'd counted the intervals -- forty seconds on, twelve off. This time, the dark lasted fourteen seconds. Then sixteen. She stopped counting."

**Template application:** Dread entries should contain at least three specific wrong details, each individually dismissible. Never name the emotion. Let the accumulation do the work.

### Creating Terror
Terror is the moment of confrontation -- when the unknown becomes known. It is powerful but transient. It requires buildup to land.

**Techniques:**
- **Short sentences after long ones.** The structural jolt creates the emotional jolt. Build a long, atmospheric sentence, then break it with something blunt. "Went out."
- **Compress time.** During terror, sentences become fragments. Paragraphs become single lines. The character does not have time to think in full sentences.
- **Use the body, not the mind.** Terror manifests physically before emotionally. Hands shake. Vision narrows. The metallic taste. Motor skills degrade -- they can't work keys, can't tie knots, can't hold objects steady.
- **One-sentence paragraphs.** After a dense block, a single-sentence paragraph hits like a punch. Do not overuse this -- it works because of rarity.

**Bad:** "She was terrified when the creature appeared."
**Good:** "The beam collapsed. Maret's leg was under it. She didn't scream -- she just looked at it."

**Template application:** Terror entries should be structurally shorter than dread entries. Use sentence fragments. The form itself communicates the state.

### Creating Unease
Unease is the uncanny valley of horror -- things that are almost right but not quite. It is subtler than dread and more persistent.

**Techniques:**
- **Familiar things behaving wrong.** A door that was closed is now open. A tool in the wrong drawer. A colleague who smiles at the wrong moments. The uncanny is triggered when the familiar becomes strange.
- **Sensory displacement.** Describe the wrong senses. Instead of what the character sees, describe what they smell or feel on their skin. Unfamiliar sensory channels create unease because readers cannot easily categorize the threat.
- **Emotional dissonance.** Characters react with calm composure to disturbing situations. The mismatch between tone and content signals something deeply wrong beneath the surface.
- **Negation as description.** Define by absence. "There was no sound from the lower level. No light. No reason to go down there." What is missing is often more disturbing than what is present.
- **The mundane detail beside the horrifying one.** "She noted the compound fracture with professional interest." Clinical precision in a horrifying context creates contrast that deepens unease.

**Bad:** "Something felt off about the room, creating an eerie atmosphere."
**Good:** "The chairs were arranged for a meeting. Eight chairs. There were only six people in the colony."

**Template application:** Unease entries should contain one concrete wrong detail embedded in otherwise normal description. The reader should have to re-read to catch it.

### Cold/Ice/Isolation Atmosphere (Frosthold-Specific)
Erebus is a frozen world that is also a living entity. The cold is not weather -- it is biology. The planet's geology is anatomy.

**Techniques:**
- **Cold as character.** Give the cold agency. It doesn't just exist -- it enters rooms, finds gaps in clothing, settles into joints. "The cold found the gap between his glove and sleeve like it had been looking for it."
- **Isolation through communication failure.** The horror of isolation is sharpest when connection almost works. A radio that picks up static that might be voices. A supply drop that was due yesterday. A distress signal that no one acknowledges.
- **The shrinking world.** Track what spaces remain habitable. Early game: the whole colony. Mid game: the heated sections. Late game: one room. The progressive contraction of safe space is itself a narrative.
- **Resource anxiety in every detail.** Characters don't just feel cold -- they calculate. How many hours of fuel? How many rations? The math of survival should permeate dialogue and thought. "Three days of fuel. Four if they stop heating the med bay."
- **White as void.** Snow and ice erase landmarks, directions, distances. Characters lose spatial orientation. The featureless white is as disorienting as darkness.

**Template application:** Every Frosthold environment description should include at least one temperature detail and one resource-scarcity detail. The cold and the counting are always present.

### Underground/Enclosed Spaces
Bore shafts, sealed sections, tunnels, the interior biology of Erebus.

**Techniques:**
- **Pressure and weight.** Characters should feel the mass above them. "Fourteen meters of ice and rock between her and the sky." The weight of the world is literal underground.
- **Sound behaves wrong.** In enclosed spaces, echoes distort. Sounds arrive from the wrong direction. Silence has a physical quality -- it presses in on the ears.
- **The transition threshold.** Mark the moment a character passes from safe space to unsafe space. A sealed door, an airlock, a point of no return. The threshold is where dread concentrates.
- **Biological architecture (Erebus-specific).** The deeper they go, the less the walls look like rock. Surfaces that are too smooth. Formations that pulse with thermal cycles. Corridors that branch like vasculature. The realization is gradual: this is not geology.
- **Limited light.** Describe what the light reveals in stages. First the floor. Then the walls. Then the thing in the corner they didn't see until they turned around.

**Template application:** Underground entries should include one claustrophobic detail (space constraint), one sensory distortion (sound/light/temperature behaving wrong), and for deep locations, one biological hint.

### Corporate Environment Horror
Mammona Corporation is the unseen antagonist. Its horror is systemic, bureaucratic, and dehumanizing.

**Techniques:**
- **Language as control.** Corporations control reality by controlling vocabulary. "Expendable assets" instead of "people." "Acceptable losses" instead of "dead colleagues." "Resource optimization" instead of "rationing." The euphemism IS the horror.
- **Calculated indifference.** The most chilling corporate evil is not malice but indifference. Mammona does not hate the colonists. Mammona does not think about them at all. They are a line item.
- **Bureaucratic formatting as horror.** A memo about staffing adjustments that is actually about letting people die. An efficiency report that quantifies human suffering in percentages. The form itself -- clean, professional, formatted -- contrasts with the content.
- **The contract.** Everything terrible was agreed to. The fine print covers Mammona. The colonists signed. The horror is that the system is working as designed.
- **Institutional memory vs. individual memory.** The corporation remembers differently than the people inside it. Official records contradict lived experience. The company version of events is always cleaner, always justified, always incomplete.

**Bad:** "Mammona was an evil corporation that didn't care about its workers."
**Good:** "MEMO: RE: Erebus Colony 7 — Status Update. Crew complement adjusted from 14 to 9 following thermal incident. Resource allocation recalculated accordingly. No impact to quarterly extraction targets. — Mammona Operations, Outer Rim Division"

**Template application:** Corporate horror entries should use corporate voice -- formal, euphemistic, data-driven. The horror is in the gap between the tone and the content. Never editorialize. Let the document speak.

## CHARACTER CRAFT

### Making Characters Feel Real
The gap between template-generated characters and real characters is the gap between categories and instances.

**The Meyer Test:** Does this description apply to one person, or could it describe a thousand? If a thousand, it is a category, not a character. Rewrite until it is an instance.

**Techniques:**
- **The diagnostic detail.** One hyper-specific behavioral quirk that feels too particular to be invented. It should be unexplained. The reader fills in the "why" themselves. "He always tapped the doorframe three times before entering a room. Not for luck -- he'd done it before Erebus." The specificity is the believability.
- **Appearance reveals psychology.** Physical description connects to how the character feels about themselves, not a camera pan. "He was five foot ten with unpredictable skin, and spent most conversations looking at his shoes." Include only details that affect behavior.
- **Behavioral flaws over adjective labels.** Replace "controlling" with "She always thinks she's the only adult in the room." The behavioral version predicts what she'll do in any situation. The adjective tells you nothing.
- **Contradiction.** Real people are inconsistent. A pacifist who keeps a loaded gun. A leader who preaches sacrifice but sleeps in the warmest room. The gap between claim and behavior is where complexity lives. The contradiction should be understandable -- the reader should imagine the internal logic without it being stated.
- **Competing motivations.** Characters feel flat when they want only one thing. Assign at least two motivations that cannot both be fully satisfied. The character's arc is which one wins, and what it costs.
- **The one thing they'd sacrifice for.** The single most important thing to know about a character is what they care about enough to suffer for. Not what they say they care about -- what they actually protect, pursue, or grieve when it's gone.

**Template application:** Every generated character needs: one diagnostic detail, one behavioral flaw (not an adjective), one contradiction, two competing motivations, and one non-negotiable value. Purge personality adjectives from character descriptions. Replace every adjective with a micro-scene or behavioral example.

### Character Voice Differentiation
If you strip the name tags from dialogue and can't tell who's speaking, the voices are not differentiated.

**Techniques:**
- **Metaphor families.** Assign each character a linguistic domain they draw metaphors from, based on background or aspiration. A soldier uses terrain and siege metaphors for feelings. A farmer says "we need to let this plan ripen." A failed academic drops literary references nobody catches. The gap between the metaphor family and the character's actual circumstances creates ironic depth.
- **Sentence rhythm as personality.** Terse, period-heavy sentences suggest survival-mode thinking, certainty, or trauma. Comma-heavy, flowing sentences suggest nuance or uncertainty. A character whose sentences get shorter under stress and longer when comfortable reveals emotional state through rhythm alone.
- **Vocabulary level.** Education, background, and self-image shape word choice. A colonist who says "Ain't nothing out there worth dying for, but I keep going out" reads differently from one who says "The risk-reward calculus doesn't favor exploration, yet I persist." Both say the same thing. Only one sounds like a person.
- **What they notice.** A scientist notices different things than a laborer. A veteran omits what a civilian would explain. Let the character's attention do the worldbuilding. A medic sees injuries first. An engineer sees structural flaws. A soldier sees exits.
- **Verbal tics and avoidances.** Small repeated patterns: a character who always says "I mean" before correcting themselves. A character who never uses anyone's name. A character who ends statements as questions. These are fingerprints, not decoration.
- **Formality gradient.** Formal speakers avoid contractions and use measured language. Casual speakers use contractions and colloquialisms. A character who shifts between registers depending on audience reveals social awareness.

**Template application:** Create tagged metaphor pools keyed to character backgrounds (military, agricultural, mechanical, medical, religious, culinary, mercantile, academic). Tag voice templates with rhythm profiles. The cover-the-names test: strip tags from generated dialogue and verify each speaker is identifiable.

### Showing Character Through Action
Never tell the reader a character is brave, kind, or ruthless. Show the behavior that earns the label.

**Techniques:**
- **Action over adjective.** "She was generous" is empty. "She gave away her last ration pack, then told the others she'd already eaten" is character.
- **The meaningful small choice.** Grand heroic moments reveal less than small, unpressured decisions. What someone does when nobody is watching, when the stakes are low, when they could easily do nothing -- that is character. A guard who checks on prisoners after lights-out. A scavenger who buries the dead he finds.
- **Signature behaviors.** Small, recurring actions that reveal values without dialogue or narration. These are the strongest characterization tools for procedural text because they work in any context.
- **Put attributes in motion.** Don't list traits; show them doing something. "He had big feet" is a measurement. "He walked hard on his big heels, as if he'd spent a lot of time in boots" is a history.
- **Response to crisis IS character.** Raid reactions, cold snaps, food shortages -- these are characterization engines. The character's behavioral posture toward crisis matters more than the crisis itself. Show it three times across different crises and it's stronger than any stated trait.

**Bad:** "Marcus was brave and loyal to his friends."
**Good:** "Marcus hadn't slept in thirty hours, but he was still out there. He'd told Kova he'd watch the north wall, and that was that."

### Backstory Revelation
The writer should know ten times more about the character than ever appears on the page.

**Techniques:**
- **The iceberg principle.** Generate full character backgrounds but only surface 10-20% in any given text. The rest informs word choice, behavioral patterns, and reaction tendencies without being stated.
- **Environmental hints over exposition.** Place unexplained objects in a character's space that suggest history without narrating it. A child's drawing pinned to the wall of a bachelor's quarters. A weapon maintained with obsessive care alongside everything else in disrepair. These details invite the reader to construct backstory themselves.
- **Family values over chronology.** "Raised by a pragmatist and a dreamer" generates more interesting behavior than "born in village, apprenticed at 14, left home at 18." Define what values were inherited and from whom.
- **Distribute across time.** Early entries reveal surface traits. Middle entries reveal motivations. Late entries reveal the deep history that explains everything. A character whose defining trauma is revealed after the reader knows their surface personality delivers retroactive recontextualization.
- **Reference past selves.** "I used to flinch at gunfire. Now I sleep through it, and that scares me more." Change requires a before-state and an after-state with an implied mechanism. The delta between the two IS the character development.

**Template application:** For characters who feature across multiple entries, distribute backstory across time. No frontloading. Each entry should raise questions the previous entry didn't answer.

### Flaws and Vulnerability
Character limitations are not decorative -- they are the load-bearing architecture of tension.

**Techniques:**
- **Traits vs. personality.** A trait is a label (brave, shy, loyal). Personality is the specific way that trait manifests. Two "brave" characters look completely different: one charges in recklessly, needing witnesses; another does the hard thing alone and tells no one.
- **Mixed good and bad.** Never generate a pure archetype. The best medic who steals food. The coward who makes everyone laugh. The rule-follower who cannot forgive. The most effective character detail is one where virtue and flaw are the same behavior seen from different angles.
- **Vulnerability makes horror work.** Without caring about the character, the reader cannot fear for them. Emotional vulnerabilities (unresolved trauma, guilt, fractured relationships) give the threat something to exploit. Physical limitations (injury, exhaustion, age) constrain options and raise stakes.
- **Invincible characters kill tension.** A character who can handle anything is a character the reader does not worry about. Competence that is genuinely useful but insufficient is the sweet spot.
- **Self-image that pressure contradicts.** Generate characters with a stated self-image that their behavior under pressure will contradict. A person who believes they are brave but freezes. A person who believes they are selfish but keeps giving things away.

### Character Under Pressure
Generated text defaults to cinematic reactions. Real people behave differently.

**Real pressure responses to generate from:**
- **Dissociation.** Eerily calm. Noticing irrelevant details. Thinking about whether they left the stove on during a life-threatening situation. This is not comedy -- it is a documented stress response.
- **Denial.** Explaining away evidence. Insisting on normal explanations past the point of plausibility. "It's just thermal expansion" is not stupidity -- it is the mind protecting itself.
- **Task fixation.** Obsessively performing a small, manageable task while ignoring the catastrophe. Carefully folding laundry while the building burns.
- **Inappropriate affect.** Laughing at funerals. Cracking jokes during crises. Becoming irritable about minor inconveniences while ignoring mortal danger.
- **Social performance.** Apologizing, using please and thank you, worrying about being rude. Social conditioning runs deeper than survival instinct.
- **Physical symptoms.** Hands shake. Stomachs churn. Vision narrows. People forget words. Motor skills degrade.

**Template application:** Build a pool of stress-response behaviors tagged by type. Draw from them when generating post-crisis text. Never default to fight-or-flight.

**Bad:** "She screamed in terror and ran."
**Good:** "She reorganized the supply closet three times today. She didn't know why. She just needed everything to be in the right place."

### Relationships and Connections
Characters exist in a social context. Remove that context and they flatten.

**Techniques:**
- **Relationship webs, not isolated character sheets.** Each character should have at least two defined relationships that create obligations, tensions, or dependencies.
- **Show relationships through asymmetry.** One person cares more. One person remembers more. One person needs the other more. Perfect symmetry is unrealistic.
- **Behavioral evidence of relationships.** Who do they eat with? Who do they check on first after an emergency? Who do they avoid? These observable patterns are more revealing than stated relationships.
- **The missing relationship.** A character who avoids connection, who eats alone, who never mentions anyone from before -- that absence is itself characterization.

## DIALOGUE

### Natural Dialogue Techniques
Real people do not speak in complete sentences, do not take turns neatly, and rarely say exactly what they mean.

**Techniques:**
- **Fragments over complete sentences.** "Three rounds left." Not "I have three rounds of ammunition remaining." People speak in shorthand, especially under stress.
- **Interruption and overlap.** People cut each other off. They talk over each other. They finish each other's sentences -- sometimes wrong.
- **Avoidance and deflection.** What characters refuse to talk about reveals more than what they say. A character who answers every question about the lower tunnels with "We should check the rations" is showing you something.
- **Repetition under stress.** People repeat themselves when afraid. "It's fine. It's fine. We're fine." The repetition IS the fear.
- **Hedging and self-correction.** "She was -- I mean, she seemed -- look, she wasn't herself." Real speech stumbles, especially around difficult subjects.
- **Contractions. Always.** "Don't" not "do not." "Can't" not "cannot." "Wouldn't" not "would not." Absence of contractions in speech is one of the strongest AI tells.

**Bad:** "I am concerned about the structural integrity of the lower levels. We should investigate before the situation deteriorates further."
**Good:** "Something's wrong down there. I don't -- look, just don't send anyone down alone, okay?"

### Genre-Specific Dialogue
Sci-fi and horror dialogue follows different rules than contemporary fiction.

**Techniques:**
- **Jargon as shorthand, not exposition.** "Realign the phase array" is better than explaining what a phase array does. Characters who understand their technology don't lecture -- they give shorthand.
- **Horror characters talk around the threat.** They use euphemism, denial, misdirection. "The situation below" instead of naming it. The gap between what they say and what they mean is where the horror lives.
- **Two engineers don't explain the reactor to each other.** They discuss whether it will hold. Dialogue reveals relationships and emotional states, not encyclopedia entries.
- **Let dialogue break under pressure.** Sentences get shorter. Pronouns lose antecedents ("It's coming" -- what is?). Characters say things that don't match what is happening (denial as dialogue).

### Dialogue as Character Revelation
What people say vs. what they mean. The subtext is the real dialogue.

**Techniques:**
- **Action during speech.** Pair quotes with physical actions: what are their hands doing, where are their eyes looking? "It's fine," she said, checking the pressure seal for the third time.
- **Let dialogue conceal rather than reveal.** Instead of "I'm scared," a character says "It's just a basement -- what's the big deal?" The gap between words and emotion creates tension.
- **The unsaid thing.** The most important line in a dialogue scene is often the one nobody speaks. Two characters discussing rations when both know someone has to go outside. Neither says it. The reader says it for them.
- **Motivation in every line.** Good dialogue happens because a character wants a result. Not self-expression -- a goal. "We should talk about what happened" is never just about talking.

### Dialogue Don'ts
These patterns reveal template origin or AI generation.

- **No "As you know, Bob."** Never have a character explain something to someone who already knows it.
- **No emotion-labeling.** "I'm scared" is almost never how people express fear. They express it through what they do, avoid, or refuse to acknowledge.
- **No exposition dumps.** If information must reach the reader, find a character who genuinely doesn't know, or deliver through action/environment.
- **No invisible dialogue tags becoming visible.** "Said" and "asked" disappear. "Exclaimed," "hissed," "growled," "breathed," "purred" draw attention to themselves. In tense scenes, drop tags entirely.
- **Avoid stock exchanges.** "You're ridiculous." / "Yes, but you love me for it." "And yet, here you are." "We're just getting started." These are AI tells.
- **No over-qualifying body language.** "She crossed her arms -- a gesture more defensive than welcoming." The interpretation should be the reader's job, not the narrator's.

## HORROR SUBTYPES

### Cosmic Horror (Lovecraftian)
The insignificance of humanity against the vast, indifferent universe. The core fear: we do not matter, and the things that do matter cannot be understood.

**Techniques:**
- **Scale beyond comprehension.** The threat is not big -- it is so vast that "big" is a meaningless word. Do not quantify. Suggest scope through the inadequacy of description. "She tried to estimate its size, but her eyes kept sliding off the edges."
- **Rational protagonists confronting the irrational.** Characters should be modern, curious, scientifically-minded -- then confront phenomena that reason cannot contain. The internal conflict between wanting logical answers and facing incomprehensible reality IS the horror.
- **Description that fails.** Lovecraft's genius was using words to convey that words are insufficient. The narrator struggles to describe what they see. Language breaks down. Analogies collapse. "It was like -- no. It wasn't like anything."
- **Pseudobibliographia.** Reference fictional texts, studies, prior expeditions as if they were real. Cross-reference between documents. This creates a fabric of reality that makes the impossible feel documented.
- **Awe alongside terror.** Cosmic horror pairs beauty with danger. The unseen can be beautiful and deadly. Cognitive dissonance between wonder and fear deepens unease.
- **Hidden realities beneath everyday life.** The surface world is a veil. Normal existence masks something darker waiting to be discovered. In Frosthold: the geology is anatomy. The planet is alive.
- **The unfamiliar threat.** Readers have recovered from vampires and ghosts. Threats that don't map to any existing category prevent the reader from deploying existing defenses. When the reader doesn't know the rules, they can't feel safe.

**Template application:** Cosmic horror entries should include at least one failed description (language breaking down), one scale indicator (vastness beyond measurement), and one rational explanation that the character clings to but the reader can see through.

### Body Horror
Transformation, contamination, loss of bodily autonomy. The core fear: your body -- the one thing you trust -- might betray you.

**Techniques:**
- **Gradual escalation.** Start with subtle anomalies. An itch that doesn't stop. A bruise that spreads too far. Escalate to grotesque transformation. The gradual progression makes it feel inevitable.
- **Multisensory description.** How it smells, sounds, feels -- not just how it looks. "The skin didn't tear -- it peeled, with a sound like pulling tape off cardboard."
- **Character denial.** Characters refusing to acknowledge what's happening to their own bodies. Treating transformations as routine. The disconnect between reality and acceptance creates psychological tension alongside physical horror.
- **Anchor in medical reality.** Draw from actual biological phenomena -- cysts, parasites, medical conditions. Grounding in recognizable biology increases both credibility and revulsion.
- **Precision over excess.** One broken finger described precisely is worse than a hundred described generically. Constant gore numbs. Strategic detail devastates.
- **Identity through the body.** The horror deepens when transformation threatens identity. Not just "my body is changing" but "I am becoming something that is not me."

**Bad:** "His body was grotesquely transformed in a horrifying display of body horror."
**Good:** "The nail on his left index finger had come off during the night. He found it in the sheets. Underneath was something smooth and dark, like chitin."

### Psychological Horror
Paranoia, gaslighting, identity erosion. The core fear: you cannot trust your own mind.

**Techniques:**
- **Unreliable information.** Present contradictory evidence. The official report says one thing; the journal says another. Let the reader decide which to trust -- and make both plausible.
- **Gradual normalcy erosion.** Track what characters lose: sleep first, then appetite, then social connection, then personal hygiene, then hope. Each stage noted in behavior, not narrated.
- **Memory as enemy.** Characters who remember things differently each time they recall them. Was the door open or closed? Did she say "stay" or "go"? The instability of memory IS the horror.
- **Isolation through perception.** The character sees/hears/knows things others don't. Are they right, or losing their mind? Don't answer. The ambiguity is the horror.
- **The sanity-check scene.** A moment where the character tests their own perception. Counts their fingers. Reads something twice. Asks someone else to confirm what they see. The act of checking is itself disturbing.

### Survival Horror
Resource scarcity, being hunted, being trapped. The core fear: there is not enough, and something is coming.

**Techniques:**
- **Math as horror.** The numbers don't work. Three days of food, five days until resupply. Two exits, one blocked. Every calculation reveals the deficit.
- **Competence under strain.** Capable characters who still can't cope. The gap between skill and situation is where dread lives. A medic who can treat wounds but not the thing that makes them.
- **The shrinking perimeter.** Track what spaces are still safe, still warm, still theirs. The progressive loss of territory is a narrative engine.
- **Resource trade-offs as moral choices.** Heat the med bay or the barracks? Send the last scout or wait blind? Every resource decision is an ethical decision.
- **The thing behind the math.** Behind every resource calculation is the unspoken question: who dies first? Characters who think it but don't say it.

### Folk Horror
Ritual, community, ancient forces. The core fear: the people you trust serve something older and worse than you imagined.

**Techniques:**
- **Community as trap.** Safety in numbers becomes imprisonment in numbers. The group's rituals, shared beliefs, and social pressure become mechanisms of horror.
- **Ancient over alien.** The threat is not from elsewhere -- it was always here. Beneath the ice, before the colony, before humanity. Discovery reveals inheritance, not invasion.
- **Ritual as normalization.** Horrifying practices presented as tradition. The characters who perform them are not evil -- they are following rules older than memory. In Frosthold: the Precursor civilization's worship of Erebus's biology.

### Quiet Horror / Creeping Dread
The mundane becoming wrong. No monsters, no gore -- just a growing certainty that something is deeply, fundamentally off.

**Techniques:**
- **Whisper, don't shout.** The horror is in the pause between words, the shadow at the edge of vision, the conversation that almost makes sense.
- **Familiar objects displaced.** A chair in the wrong place. A tool returned clean when it was left dirty. Objects that have moved without agency.
- **Pattern and pattern-break.** Establish a routine. Let the reader settle into it. Then change one element. "Every morning, the condensation on the window formed the same pattern. Until Thursday."
- **The calm final entry.** After escalating panic in a series of logs, a final entry that is eerily calm implies the worst kind of resolution. "Everything is fine now. I understand."
- **Subtext over text.** What isn't said still screams. End scenes on gestures rather than resolutions. Allow conversations to trail off. Reveal reactions before their causes.

### Corporate Horror
Bureaucratic evil, expendability, systems designed to consume people.

**Techniques:**
- **The system working as designed.** The horror is not that the system has broken -- it's that the system was built to produce this outcome. Mammona's contracts, quotas, and resource allocations function perfectly. That's the problem.
- **Language sanitizing atrocity.** "Personnel adjustment" for death. "Resource reallocation" for theft. "Acceptable casualty parameters" for murder. The euphemism is both tool and evidence.
- **Individual vs. institution.** A Mammona employee who privately acknowledges the horror but continues working. Not a villain -- a person trapped in a system bigger than their conscience.
- **Data as dehumanization.** People reduced to numbers on a spreadsheet. Colonist 7 becomes Entry 7 becomes a data point in a quarterly report. The progressive abstraction is the horror.
- **Forms and procedures during crisis.** The requirement to fill out an incident report while people are dying. Bureaucracy as a force that continues grinding regardless of human urgency.

## FOUND DOCUMENTS (Datapads/Logs)

### General Principles for All Document Types
The document format IS the storytelling technique. Epistolary fiction goes beyond "show, don't tell" and forces the writer to only "show."

**Core rules:**
- **Ask: "Why is the character writing this?"** A journal entry during a crisis needs a reason to exist. People write to process, to document, to confess, to leave evidence. The reason shapes the voice.
- **Ask: "Would they write this?"** A panicking person doesn't compose eloquent paragraphs. A scientist doesn't write like a poet. Match voice to document type and emotional state.
- **The writer is a character.** The document's author has habits, blind spots, biases. A military officer omits emotional detail. A scientist over-qualifies every statement. The voice tells you who is writing before you know their name.
- **Use format as meaning.** Time gaps between entries suggest events too traumatic to record. Entries that reference earlier entries create internal continuity. Crossed-out text reveals what someone tried to hide.

### Research Logs
Scientific voice deteriorating under pressure.

**Pattern:** Start formal and precise. Introduce anomalies as "inconsistencies." Let professional doubt shade into personal fear. The transition from passive voice ("was observed") to active first person ("I saw") marks the moment science fails the writer.

**Good example progression:**
1. "Sample 47-B: Anomalous crystalline formation at 12m depth. Composition analysis pending. Note: ambient temperature inconsistent with expected subsurface thermal gradient."
2. "Sample 47-B update: Composition analysis returned ERROR three times. Re-calibrated equipment. Same result. The crystal is not registering as any known mineral. Requesting second opinion from Dr. Voss."
3. "Voss is gone. Equipment keeps returning ERROR. I tested my own blood as a control and it came back normal, so the machine works. The crystal doesn't. Or it does, and I don't understand what it's telling me."

### Personal Journals
Intimate voice, observation, change over time.

**Pattern:** Contractions, sentence fragments, emotional language, self-correction. The writer argues with themselves, contradicts previous entries, avoids certain subjects then suddenly confronts them.

**Techniques:**
- **Self-deception on the page.** "Tried to sleep. Couldn't. Kept hearing -- no, I'm imagining things." The dash and correction reveal more than a direct statement would.
- **Mundane anchors.** Journals mention meals, weather, minor irritations alongside horror. The mundane details are the normalcy the writer is clinging to.
- **Time erosion.** Early entries are dated precisely. Later entries lose track. "Day something. Doesn't matter."
- **The unsent letter within the journal.** An entry that shifts from diary to direct address -- talking to someone who isn't there. A dead colleague, a family member, themselves.

### Corporate Memos
Bureaucratic horror through language control.

**Pattern:** Formal, clipped, passive voice, euphemism. Numbered action items. Distribution lists. Classification levels. The horror is in the gap between the professional formatting and the human cost of the content.

**Techniques:**
- **Euphemism as weapon.** "The asset was neutralized" for murder. "Colony optimization achieved" for abandonment. "Personnel surplus corrected" for death.
- **Classification levels as mystery.** A partially redacted memo implies that what's visible is bad enough -- what's hidden is worse.
- **Distribution lists as characterization.** Who received this memo? Who was excluded? The cc line is a power map.
- **Action items as horror.** "ACTION REQUIRED: Seal Sublevel 4 access. Remove all personnel records for Sublevel 4 team from colony manifest. Report adjusted headcount to Operations."

### Medical Reports
Clinical precision dissolving into something else.

**Pattern:** Start with standard medical formatting -- patient ID, vitals, presenting symptoms. Let the language strain as the condition defies classification. The moment the medic starts writing outside medical vocabulary is the moment the horror arrives.

**Good example:** "Patient presents with dermal anomaly, left forearm. Initial assessment: allergic reaction or contact dermatitis. UPDATE: Reclassifying. The growth is not inflammation. It has structure. It has symmetry. It looks -- this isn't a medical term, but it looks intentional."

### Audio Transcripts
Found-footage techniques in text form.

**Techniques:**
- **Notational conventions as atmosphere.** [static], [unintelligible], [pause -- 14 seconds], [background noise: rhythmic, unidentified]. The brackets contain the horror.
- **Speaker identification breaking down.** "SPEAKER 1:" in early entries becomes "UNKNOWN:" becomes "[no speaker identified]."
- **Ambient sound as narrative.** What the microphone picks up that the speakers don't address. "[Background: scraping sound, consistent, increasing volume. No speaker acknowledges.]"
- **Transcript ending without conclusion.** The recording continues after meaningful speech stops. "[Silence -- 4 minutes 17 seconds. Recording ends.]" What happened in that silence?

### Unsent Letters
Emotional arc, the unsaid, the human behind the colonist.

**Pattern:** Written to someone who will never read it. The writer knows this. The letter is really for themselves -- a way to feel connected to someone beyond the ice.

**Techniques:**
- **The impossible update.** Telling someone about events they can't possibly help with. "You'd hate the food here. You'd hate everything here. But you'd find a way to make it funny."
- **The confession.** Things the writer would never say in person. "I told them I volunteered. I didn't. I just couldn't find a reason to say no."
- **The goodbye that isn't labeled as one.** A letter that is clearly a final communication but never says so directly. The writer talks about future plans they both know won't happen.

## NARRATIVE TECHNIQUES

### Subtext and Implication
What's NOT said is scarier than what is. The reader's imagination always produces something more personally terrifying than the writer can provide.

**Techniques:**
- **The 4:1 ratio.** For every direct encounter with a threat, generate four indirect references -- near-misses, ambiguous evidence, secondhand reports, environmental wrongness. "I thought I heard something in the ventilation shaft but it was probably just thermal expansion" is more unsettling than "The creature attacked."
- **Shadow profiles.** Details about a threat conveyed only through other characters' reactions. "Kova won't talk about what happened at Outpost 7. She just checks the locks three times now." The behavioral evidence characterizes the threat better than direct description.
- **The unsaid question.** Two characters discussing rations, and neither says what both are thinking: who goes outside?
- **Reaction before cause.** Show the response before showing what caused it. A character flinches before the reader knows what they saw. The gap creates dread.
- **Trust gaps and silence.** End scenes on gestures rather than resolutions. Allow conversations to trail off. The absence of closure implies the worst.

### Pacing in Short Form
Tension curves for 5-7 entry datapads and log sequences.

**The tension formula:** Tension = unresolved stakes + time. A character waiting in silence isn't scary unless we know what might break that silence.

**Structure for a 5-entry datapad sequence:**
1. **Establishment.** Normal operations. One small wrong detail buried in mundane reporting.
2. **First crack.** The small wrong detail is acknowledged. Rational explanation offered. Voice remains professional.
3. **Escalation.** Multiple wrong details. Rational explanations strain. Voice shifts from professional to personal.
4. **Crisis.** Direct confrontation with the threat/truth. Entries get shorter. Fragments appear. Time stamps may become erratic.
5. **Resolution (choose one):**
   a. Silence. No final entry. What happened?
   b. False calm. "Everything is fine now." (The worst option.)
   c. Truncation. Entry cuts off mid-sentence. "I can hear it in the--"
   d. Recontextualization. Final entry reveals the first entry meant something different than the reader thought.

**Pacing controls:**
- **Slow for dread:** Long sentences, long paragraphs, environmental detail, backstory.
- **Fast for terror:** Short sentences, action verbs, fragments, single-line paragraphs.
- **The transition is the weapon.** The shift from slow to fast creates the jolt. Build atmosphere with three long paragraphs, then break it with two words.

### The Twist
Recontextualization is always stronger than surprise.

**Techniques:**
- **Recontextualization over surprise.** A twist that makes the reader re-read everything before it. The first entry seemed like routine logging. After the final entry, it's clearly a farewell.
- **Plant details that pay off.** Mention something specific in entry 1 that becomes significant in entry 5. The reader didn't notice it the first time. Now they can't forget it.
- **The twist that changes WHO, not WHAT.** The events don't change -- the reader's understanding of who was doing them changes. The log they've been reading was written by the thing they thought was the threat.
- **Avoid the cheat twist.** Information withheld purely for surprise, with no clues planted earlier, feels like a lie. The reader should be able to look back and see the signs.

### Foreshadowing
Planting details that pay off later without telegraphing the payoff.

**Techniques:**
- **The innocuous specific.** A detail that seems like characterization but is actually setup. "She always carried the red toolkit, even on days she wasn't on repair duty." Why? The reader finds out later.
- **Environmental echoes.** A sound, a temperature, a smell that appears in a safe context early and returns in a dangerous context later. The recall triggers unease before the reader consciously connects the moments.
- **The self-contradicting record.** Early entries mention a fact. Later entries contradict it. Which version is true? The contradiction is the foreshadowing -- someone is unreliable, or reality is unstable.

### Unreliable Information
When the narrator, the document, or the system lies.

**Techniques:**
- **Build trust first, then betray it.** An unreliable narrator has maximum impact if the reader believed them. Keep details accurate early. Let inconsistencies creep in. The "aha" moment is more powerful after investment.
- **Multiple contradicting sources.** The official report says the mission succeeded. The personal journal says everyone is dead. The reader must choose -- and that uncertainty is the horror.
- **Subtle inconsistencies.** Small enough that readers might miss them on first reading, but obvious enough for the "aha" when truth is revealed. A character mentions a detail in entry 3 that contradicts entry 1.
- **Types of unreliability:** The liar (knows the truth, conceals it). The madman (cannot perceive truth). The naif (lacks context to interpret what they see). The system (HERMES reporting data that serves Mammona's interests, not the colonists').
- **The document that contradicts what the reader knows.** The most effective found documents present information that conflicts with what the reader has learned from other sources. Which document is trustworthy?

## PROCEDURAL GENERATION CRAFT

### Template Variety Techniques
How to make templates not feel templated.

**Techniques:**
- **Syntactic variation.** The same information can be structured five different ways. "He checked the seal" / "The seal. He checked it again" / "Seal check: nominal" / "First thing, always -- the seal" / "He'd already checked the seal twice." Pool all five. Rotate.
- **Entry point variation.** Don't always start the same way. Some entries start with dialogue, some with action, some with environment, some mid-thought. Vary the first three words across pool entries. If multiple entries start with "The" or "I," restructure.
- **Detail substitution depth.** Replace not just nouns but sentence structures. Swapping "cold" for "freezing" is surface variation. Swapping "It was cold" for "Her breath came out in sheets" is structural variation.
- **The Meyer test for pools.** After generating a pool entry, attempt to swap it into a different character's profile. If it fits, it's too generic. Rewrite until it could only belong to one person.
- **Voice consistency within, variety between.** A single character's entries should sound consistent. Different characters' entries should sound distinct. The consistency proves the character; the variety proves the pool.

### Cross-Referencing for Emergent Narrative
How connections between generated elements create story.

**Techniques:**
- **Name echoes.** A character mentioned in one datapad appears in another. A location referenced in a research log shows up in a personal journal. Cross-references create the illusion of a coherent world.
- **Contradicting accounts.** Two different entries describe the same event differently. The player must reconcile them. This is emergent mystery -- no author needed.
- **Temporal layering.** Entries from different time periods describing the same location. What changed? What stayed the same? The delta tells a story.
- **The absent character.** A person referenced in multiple entries who never appears directly. The player constructs them from fragments. This character, built by the player's imagination, is often more compelling than any directly described.

### Anti-Repetition Strategies
Pool sizing, syntactic variation, deduplication.

**Techniques:**
- **Minimum pool depth.** No pool should have fewer than 15 entries for any element the player will see more than twice. 30+ for common elements. Repetition is the fastest way to break immersion.
- **Structural fingerprinting.** After generating a pool, check the first three words of each entry. If more than 20% share an opening pattern, restructure. Check sentence counts -- if all entries are 3 sentences, vary between 2-5.
- **Semantic deduplication.** Two entries that say the same thing in different words are still repetitive. Each pool entry must contain at least one idea, detail, or implication that no other entry in the pool contains.
- **Category coverage.** For character traits, ensure the pool covers multiple domains: physical, behavioral, social, emotional, professional. A pool of 20 entries that are all physical descriptions is effectively a pool of one type repeated.

### The DF/RimWorld Standard
What makes those generators great, and how to replicate it.

**Principles:**
- **Simulation over scripting.** The story emerges from systems interacting, not from authored sequences. Characters have autonomy shaped by personality, mood, and circumstance.
- **Apophenia is a feature.** Minimal representation lets players project meaning. A colonist's mood drop after a death isn't scripted grief -- but the player reads it as grief, and it becomes real.
- **Cascading event chains.** One event triggers consequences that spawn additional complications. A failed hunt leads to food shortage leads to mood drop leads to mental break leads to fire leads to injury. The chain is the story.
- **Consequence permanence.** Deaths, injuries, and relationship changes persist. Characters remember. The weight of history accumulates.
- **Narrative ellipsis.** Critical events occur off-screen or as text summaries. Expeditions become invisible points on a map. The gap between what happened and what the player knows creates dramatic tension.
- **Character semi-autonomy.** Characters don't do exactly what the player tells them. Mood, personality, and physical state intervene. The gap between player intention and character action is where emergent narrative lives.
- **Modular narrator pacing.** Different difficulty/story settings change event frequency and severity. The player customizes story density. The system provides unpredictability within chosen parameters.

## ANTI-PATTERNS (What NOT to Do)

### AI Writing Tells
Comprehensive condensed reference. If any of these appear in a pool entry, rewrite it.

**Flagged Vocabulary (immediate rewrite):**
- Verbs: delve, underscore, showcase, foster, leverage, bolster, garner, encompass, resonate, embark, unravel, cultivate, utilize, facilitate, endeavor, grapple
- Adjectives: crucial, pivotal, vital, intricate, meticulous, vibrant, enduring, robust, comprehensive, paramount, nuanced, profound, transformative, multifaceted, myriad, ethereal, luminous, pristine, palpable, tangible
- Abstract nouns (figurative): tapestry, landscape, realm, paradigm, testament, interplay, beacon, cornerstone, mosaic, symphony, hallmark
- Adverbs (overused): moreover, furthermore, additionally, notably, consequently, subsequently, ultimately, indeed

**Flagged Structures (immediate rewrite):**
- "It's not just X, it's Y" -- THE most identified AI tell
- "Not only X, but also Y"
- Rule of three used repeatedly (triplet adjectives, triplet phrases)
- Em dash overuse (1-2 per page is normal; 5+ is a tell)
- Copula avoidance: "serves as" / "stands as" replacing "is"
- Scene-ending introspective summary: "As he looked out at the cold night, he wondered..."
- "Maybe, just maybe" / "It wasn't perfect. But for now, it was enough"

**Flagged Fiction Patterns (immediate rewrite):**
- "Breath hitched" / "heart raced" / "jaw clenched" / "chill ran down spine"
- "Casting long shadows" / "dimly lit" / "dust motes"
- "Beacon of hope" / "weight of [abstract concept]" / "tapestry of"
- Constant humming, smirking, sighing, gazing
- "With practiced ease" / "barely above a whisper"
- "The smile didn't quite reach her eyes"

**Flagged Names (never use):**
- Elara (across ALL LLMs), Elena, Sarah Chen, Marcus, Lily
- Blackwood, Thorne, Zephyr, Dr. Aris Thorne

**Flagged Dialogue (never use):**
- "I don't know what I'd do without you" / "You'll never have to find out"
- "You're ridiculous/impossible" / "Yes, but you love me for it"
- "And yet, here you are" / "We're just getting started"
- Toast patterns: "Here's to X. May we Y"

**Structural Tells:**
- Scenes ending with character reflecting instead of acting
- Show AND Tell (describing action then explaining what it means)
- Excessive consistency -- no quirks, no errors, no contractions
- Overly balanced "on one hand / on the other hand" constructions
- "Little did they know..." prophetic transitions

### Horror Cliches to Avoid

- **The creepy child.** Unless you have a specific, original take, this is exhausted.
- **The jump scare with no setup.** Shock without tension is just noise.
- **The explained monster.** The moment you fully explain the threat, you convert dread into a problem to be solved. Delay revelation.
- **The protagonist who investigates the scary noise alone.** If you must do this, give them a believable reason.
- **Pure evil without motivation.** Villains who are evil for evil's sake feel flat. Even Erebus has biology, not malice.
- **The instant expert.** A character who immediately knows what the threat is and how to fight it kills all mystery.
- **The death that doesn't land.** A character who dies before the reader has invested in them is not tragic -- they're a prop.
- **Constant violence.** Violence numbs. Strategic violence devastates. Earn your gore.
- **The happy ending that betrays the genre.** Resolving dark themes too quickly or neatly. Horror should leave marks.

### Sci-Fi Cliches to Avoid

- **Technobabble paragraphs.** Characters who understand technology don't lecture about it. They use shorthand.
- **The info-dump opening.** "In the year 2590, humanity had expanded to..." Show the world through character experience, not narrator exposition.
- **Hand-wavy science presented as hard science.** If you must hand-wave, do it briefly and with conviction. Don't draw attention to the gap.
- **The world-explanation conversation.** Two colonists do not explain the colony to each other. They live there.
- **Generic future-slang.** Invented slang should feel organic, not try-hard. If it doesn't arise naturally from the world, cut it.

### Dialogue Cliches
Lines that reveal template origin.

- Any line that could appear in a different genre without modification is too generic.
- Emotional summaries: "I just feel so..." -- show the emotion through behavior.
- Perfect articulation under pressure. Real people under stress lose vocabulary, repeat themselves, trail off.
- Uniform sentence length across all speakers. Different characters have different rhythms.
- Absence of contractions in casual speech. This is the single most flagrant AI tell in dialogue.

## WORD-LEVEL CRAFT

### Strong Verbs
Replace weak verbs with specific ones that carry their own imagery.

**Principle:** If a verb needs an adverb, it's the wrong verb. "Walked quickly" should be "strode" or "rushed" or "scrambled." Each replacement carries different connotations -- choose the one that fits.

**Common replacements for horror/sci-fi:**

| Weak | Strong Options (choose by context) |
|------|------|
| walked | trudged, staggered, crept, shuffled, limped |
| looked | glanced, squinted, peered, scanned, stared |
| said | muttered, rasped, whispered (sparingly), snapped |
| went | fled, retreated, slipped, ducked, bolted |
| was scared | froze, flinched, recoiled, locked up |
| held | gripped, clutched, cradled, pinned |
| hit | cracked, slammed, drove, hammered |
| moved | shifted, lurched, darted, inched |
| fell | collapsed, dropped, buckled, pitched |

**Caution:** Over-using dramatic verbs is as bad as under-using them. "Said" is invisible and often correct. Not every walk is a trudge. Match verb intensity to scene intensity.

### Sensory Hierarchy
Which senses create which effects. Most generated text defaults to visual-only.

**Visual (sight):** The default sense. Establishes setting, scale, and spatial relationships. Overused in generated text. Necessary but insufficient alone.

**Auditory (sound):** Creates atmosphere and threat awareness. Sounds the character cannot identify are inherently unsettling. The absence of expected sound (silence where there should be machinery) is more disturbing than unexpected noise. "The generator had been humming for three weeks. She didn't notice until it stopped."

**Olfactory (smell):** The most memory-linked sense. Smell bypasses rational processing and triggers emotional responses directly. Use smell to connect present moments to past trauma or safety. "The corridor smelled like the inside of a body -- warm metal and something organic." Smell is underused in generated text and disproportionately effective.

**Tactile (touch):** Creates immediacy and collapses distance between reader and character. Temperature, texture, pressure, pain. Touch prevents the reader from remaining detached. "The wall was warm. Fourteen meters underground, behind insulated paneling, and the wall was warm."

**Gustatory (taste):** The most intimate sense. Usually reserved for significant moments. The metallic taste of fear. The flat taste of recycled water. The wrongness of food that should taste familiar but doesn't.

**Proprioception / vestibular (body position, balance):** Often overlooked. Vertigo, disorientation, the sense of falling, the inability to tell which way is up. Powerful in underground/zero-gravity scenarios. "She couldn't tell if the floor was tilting or if she was."

**Template application:** Every scene or entry should engage at least two senses, and one of them should not be sight. For horror, prioritize sound and smell -- they signal threats the character (and reader) cannot yet see. For body horror, prioritize touch and proprioception.

### Sentence Rhythm
Short for impact, long for atmosphere, fragments for urgency.

**The three rhythms:**
- **Long, winding sentences:** Build atmosphere, delay revelation, create suspense. Force the reader to stay in the discomfort. Use for dread, for environmental description, for the approach of something terrible.
- **Short sentences:** Deliver impact. Punctuate. Stop the reader cold. Use after long buildups for maximum contrast. Use during action, crisis, revelation.
- **Fragments:** Communicate deterioration, urgency, or a mind under pressure. A narrator who can no longer form complete sentences is showing you their state without telling you. "The readings were nominal across all sectors. All green. All fine. Fine."

**The transition is the technique.** The shift from long to short creates the jolt. Three winding atmospheric sentences, then: "It stopped." That two-word sentence carries the weight of the entire buildup.

**One-sentence paragraphs:** Use as a beat. After a dense block of text, a single sentence standing alone hits like a fist. Overuse kills the effect -- save it for moments that earn it.

**Repetition with variation:** Establish a pattern, then break it. "The door was locked. The next door was locked. The next door was open." The break in pattern is where the fear lives.

**Template application:** Vary sentence count and length across pool entries. If all entries are three sentences of similar length, the pool has a rhythm problem. Mix 2-sentence entries with 5-sentence entries. Mix fragments with compound sentences.

### Concrete vs. Abstract
Specific details always beat generalities. This is the single most important word-level principle.

**The specificity ladder:**
1. Abstract: "It was cold." (worthless)
2. General: "The temperature had dropped." (functional but flat)
3. Specific: "The temperature read minus forty-two." (concrete)
4. Experiential: "Her breath froze in her eyelashes before she blinked." (character in the world)

Always aim for level 3 or 4. Level 1 and 2 are placeholders, not prose.

**Concrete motivation over abstract motivation:**
- Abstract: "She wanted freedom." (meaningless)
- Concrete: "She couldn't afford pencils for her children." (a person)

**Concrete detail over category descriptor:**
- Category: "He talked slowly." (could be anyone)
- Instance: "When he talked, you could watch grass grow." (one person)

**Concrete fear over labeled fear:**
- Label: "She was afraid." (tells nothing)
- Concrete: "She kept checking the pressure gauge. Green. Still green. She checked it again." (shows everything)

**Template application:** After generating any text, scan for abstract nouns and adjective-labels. Replace each one with a concrete detail or observable behavior. "She felt guilty" becomes "She volunteered for every dangerous shift that week." "He was afraid" becomes "He'd started sleeping with the lights on, and he didn't talk about why."

## SCI-FI WORLDBUILDING

### Through Character Perspective
The world should be revealed through what characters notice, take for granted, and misunderstand.

**Techniques:**
- **Let characters take things for granted.** If technology is mundane to the character, do not explain it. "She keyed the cycler and waited for pressure to equalize" requires no footnote. Readers accept what characters accept.
- **Sprinkle, don't pour.** Worldbuild through action, dialogue, and incidental detail -- never through exposition blocks. A character cursing a malfunctioning tool teaches the reader the tech exists, that it breaks, and that the character depends on it -- all in one beat.
- **What they notice reveals who they are.** A scientist sees anomalies. A soldier sees exits. An engineer sees stress fractures. The character's attention IS the worldbuilding.
- **The two-part premise.** "What if?" creates the concept. "And then what would happen?" creates the human consequences. The second question is where the story lives. "What if the planet is alive?" is a concept. "What happens to the miners who just drilled into its nervous system?" is a story.
- **Show world through constraint.** Worldbuilding that matters is worldbuilding that changes what a character can or must do. If a world detail doesn't constrain or enable a character's choices, cut it.

## CHARACTER INTRODUCTION (BCI Framework)

On first appearance, establish three things fast:

1. **Believe** -- one specific detail that makes them real, not a type. A sensory detail, a behavioral quirk, something too particular to be generic.
2. **Care** -- a circumstance that creates investment. Doesn't require victimhood; competence under pressure works. A character who is good at something the situation demands but still at risk.
3. **Invest** -- evidence they can act on their own behalf. A demonstrated skill, a resourceful choice, a moment of agency. The reader wants to watch this person solve problems.

**Template application:** Character introductions should contain all three BCI elements within the first two sentences. One concrete sensory detail, one pressure or stakes indicator, one competence signal.

**Bad:** "Marcus was a tall, strong colonist with brown eyes. He was brave and kind."
**Good:** "Marcus had already inventoried the supply closet twice by the time the rest of the crew woke up -- hands shaking, but the count was perfect."

## HORROR CHARACTER ARCHETYPES AND SUBVERSION

Characters who serve horror-narrative functions should be built as people first, archetypes second.

| Archetype | Subversion |
|-----------|-----------|
| The Final Survivor | Give them a reason to survive that isn't just survival. An obligation, a promise, unfinished business. |
| The Skeptic | Make them right about everything except the supernatural. Their rationality is an asset everywhere else. |
| The Expert | Give them knowledge that is mostly correct but flawed in one critical way -- due to personal history, not ignorance. |
| The Sacrifice | Give them a future -- a project, a reunion, a confession they were building toward. The interrupted future is what makes death land. |
| The Authority Figure | Make them competent at everything except the thing that matters. Their normal-situation leadership is excellent. |
| The Innocent | Let them protect someone else at a critical moment. The protected becoming the protector. |
| The Traitor | Make them the most practically useful group member until the betrayal. The dependence makes betrayal devastating. |

## RULES OF THUMB -- Master List

### Horror Rules
1. The reader's imagination is worse than yours. Suggest. Do not explain.
2. Horror is character under pressure. Without character, there is no horror -- just shock.
3. The unseen is scarier than the seen. The unexplained is more disturbing than the explained.
4. Earn your reveals. Every revelation should be paid for with buildup.
5. End on the wrong note. The most disturbing endings feel almost normal.
6. Restraint over excess. One precise detail beats a hundred generic ones.
7. Reverse tropes. The familiar inverted is more unsettling than the wholly original.
8. Normalcy is a resource that depletes. Track its loss.
9. Indirect evidence outperforms direct description.
10. Real stress looks wrong. People laugh, fixate on trivia, apologize to attackers.

### Character Rules
1. The person before the peril. Build the character, then apply the horror.
2. Vulnerability is structure. Limitations are load-bearing, not decorative.
3. Compelling beats likeable. Specific desires and methods beat generic goodness.
4. The villain is a character too. Same depth, same specificity.
5. Competence makes horror worse. The capable person who still can't cope.
6. Subvert through specificity. Too particular, too individual, too real to be a type.
7. Show, never tell. Replace every adjective with a behavior.
8. The gap between self-image and action. That's where depth lives.

### Sci-Fi Rules
1. Accuracy is an aspect of quality, not the whole. Much can be forgiven in a well-told tale.
2. Research your premise deeply, your props lightly.
3. Worldbuild through constraint. Show what the world prevents.
4. Vary your registers. Poetic builds atmosphere. Blunt delivers impact.
5. Form is content. The document's structure tells its own story.
6. Characters who understand technology don't explain it. They use it.

### Procedural Generation Rules
1. Specific > generic. Always. The Meyer test: could this describe a thousand people?
2. Vary syntax, not just vocabulary. Structural variation beats word substitution.
3. Cross-reference for emergent narrative. Connections create story.
4. The 4:1 ratio. Four indirect horror references per one direct encounter.
5. Mystery preservation. For every three facts, one unanswered question.
6. Pool depth matters. 15+ entries minimum for anything seen twice. 30+ for common elements.
7. The cover-the-names test. Dialogue should be identifiable without tags.
8. Consequence permanence. What happened stays happened.

- [ ] Backstory distributed across time, not frontloaded

### Antagonist/Threat Checklist

- [ ] Full profile generated with only 30-40% surfaced

- [ ] Physical details connected to origin/history
- [ ] Shadow profile (conveyed only through others' reactions)
- [ ] Lasting impact defined (permanent changes after encounter)
- [ ] Deliberate mystery gaps (unanswered questions)
